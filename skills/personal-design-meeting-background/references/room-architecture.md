# Room Architecture

Design the room in plan first, then in elevation, then decorate. A prop list written straight onto a flat wall produces a stage flat — everything at one depth, evenly spaced, obviously fake. Everything here exists to stop that.

## Build a corner

The single biggest structural upgrade. Do not describe one wall facing the camera. Describe a **corner**: a window wall running away from the camera in perspective on one side, and a back wall square to the camera.

```
   PLAN (from above)                    the corner does the work:
                                        the window wall converges,
   ╔══════════════════════════════╗     the back wall stays square,
   ║  anchor        shelving      ║     and the room has depth before
   ║                    ▓         ║     a single object is placed
 w ║                    ▓         ║
 i ║                    ▓         ║
 n ║                              ║
 d ║                              ║
 o ╚══════════════════════════════╝
 w      ▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
        desk (cropped by frame)
              ▲
            camera
```

Perspective gives you depth for free. Two receding lines — the window wall and the desk edge — do more than any amount of described "depth of field".

Put the window on the left by default. Light entering from frame left, shelving in the light on the right.

## Stagger the furniture in depth

A corner gives the *walls* depth. It does nothing for the furniture, and a room where every readable thing sits on the back wall is still a shallow box — a stage flat with a desk in front of it.

Legibility is a function of distance, and **you can move the furniture, not just the objects on it.** Anything meant to be read goes on the nearest piece.

```
   PLAN — three furniture planes, not one wall

   ╔══════════════════════════════════════╗
   ║   far: window + view      back wall  ║
   ║                     anchor           ║
   ║                                      ║
   ║        mid: identity object, desk    ║
   ║                              ┌───┐   ║
   ║                              │ N │   ║  near unit, angled,
   ║   ▓ soft foreground          │ E │   ║  cropped by the right
   ║     plant, cropped           │ A │   ║  edge — carries every
   ╚══════════════════════════════│ R │═══╝  named title
                    ▲             └───┘
                  camera
```

- **Near plane — build a bump-out, don't angle a bookcase.** A freestanding unit turned toward the camera reads wrong: it floats, its perspective fights the room, and it looks like furniture dropped into a photograph. Instead project a section of the back wall forward — a structural pier, a chimney breast, a closet return — and hang the shelves flat on its face. The shelves stay square to camera and perfectly readable; the pier's narrow *return* side gives you the receding line for free. Crop it at the frame edge so only part of its width shows.

  This also frames the anchor: the main wall now reads as a recessed alcove bracketed by the window wall on one side and the pier on the other.

- **Open shelves, not a cabinet.** Planks on slim brackets, no back panel and no sides, so the wall texture shows between and behind every shelf and the concealed strip lights can rake the wall as well as the spines. A closed carcass turns the near plane into a heavy brown block. Keep the pier narrow — a wide one is just an empty column.
- **Mid plane** — the desk, the identity object, the back wall and its anchor.
- **Far plane** — the window and the view beyond it.
- **Foreground** — one element very close to the lens at the opposite edge, heavily out of focus and cropped: a cascading plant, a door frame, the back of a chair. This is the strongest depth cue available and it costs one clause.

Focus deep enough to hold the near unit *and* the back wall — around f/5.6, focused between them. State it as "sharp from the near shelving through to the back wall".

Cropping the near unit is not a loss. A bookcase fully in frame is a distant bookcase.

Hold the near unit to about a quarter of the frame width and keep the camera distance stated. Given a near plane and no distance, a generator will step back to fit the room in, and the whole frame becomes an architectural wide shot with no foreground and nowhere for the person to sit.

## Three horizontal registers

Real rooms have horizontal structure. Objects that sit *on* shared lines read as designed; objects at random heights read as clutter.

```
   ELEVATION (what the camera sees)

   ─────────────────────────────────────  ceiling register  (top ~15%)
      exposed pipe, top rail of the shelving, duct, ceiling joist

   ─────────────────────────────────────  shelf register    (~35–60%)
      THE READABLE BAND — anchor, books, letterboards,
      identity object, brand marks. Everything meant to
      be read lives between these two lines.

   ─────────────────────────────────────  desk register     (bottom ~25%)
      cropped desk edge, mug, coaster, notebook, cable
```

The **ceiling register** is what tells the eye this is a building and not a backdrop. A run of black pipe, a duct, the top rail of the shelving. Cheap and it works. Use structure, not a light fitting — a hanging bulb up there becomes a hot spot rather than a ceiling.

The **shelf register** is where the portrait actually happens. If it isn't in this band, nobody reads it.

The **desk register** is foreground and it's the only part that should be out of focus.

## The center anchor

One large, bright rectangle on the back wall, centered or just off-center: a whiteboard with a hand-drawn framework, or a big hand-lettered print.

**Hang it, don't inset it.** Say it is a slim-framed board sitting on the face of the wall with a marker tray, and say there is wall visible above, below and to either side. Left vague, generators build it into the wall as a recessed panel — which flattens the wall, reads as a projection screen, and swallows the surface the raking light needs to work on. Keep it wider than it is tall and no more than about a third of the frame.

**Let the mid-ground objects overlap its lower edge.** A bike's bars crossing the bottom corner of the board reads as a room somebody uses. A board floating in clean isolation reads as a showroom. Protect the upper two thirds; give the bottom third away.

It does three jobs:

1. **Value contrast.** The person sits against it, so their head and shoulders separate cleanly.
2. **Content.** It carries the highest-value readable thing in the room.
3. **Structure.** It stops the composition drifting left or right.

Hang it so its **lower third is expendable** — that's the part the head covers. Put the title row, the top boxes, and the loudest content in its upper two-thirds.

A bright rectangle behind the head is correct and desirable. What is not correct is a *hard vertical edge* landing exactly where the shoulders are — segmentation tears along those. Size and place the anchor so its left and right edges sit outside the shoulder line, or well inside it.

## The head zone

The person occupies a wedge in the **bottom center** — roughly the middle third of the width, from the desk line up to about four-fifths of the frame height. Not a full-height column.

- Small readable detail placed there is wasted. It gets covered.
- Large bright shapes placed there are good. Only their lower portion is lost.
- Keep hard vertical edges and bright point sources out of it.

This is why a centered whiteboard works and a centered floor lamp does not.

**The desk has a head zone too, and it is easy to forget.** The torso covers the middle of the desk as surely as the head covers the middle of the wall. The mug, the notebook, and anything else meant to be seen belong at the **outer edges of the picture**, and the middle third of the desk stays bare.

**Position against the frame, never against the furniture.** "At the left of the desk" is not an instruction — a generator satisfies it by placing the object left of something else, which is how a notebook ends up dead centre. Say *in the left quarter of the picture*, *hard against the right edge of the frame*, *the middle third of the desk completely bare*. Frame-relative phrasing is the only kind that survives, and it is the difference between a mug someone can see and a mug behind a shoulder. Keep objects clear of the very bottom-left corner too, where the platform prints the name badge — see `on-camera.md`. A beautiful stack of legible spines centred on the desk is a stack nobody will ever see.

## Flanking verticals

Bracket the frame with two strong verticals: the window frame and mullions on one side, tall shelving on the other. They stop the eye walking out of the picture.

Near-symmetry is fine here. These are composed rooms, not candid ones, and a balanced frame reads as considered rather than templated. The asymmetry comes from the light and the object grouping, not from lopsided furniture.

## Depth stacking

On the shelving side, give objects **three or four distinct depths**:

- back of the shelf — framed pieces, spines leaning against the upright
- mid — the stacked books, the brand bottle
- front lip — a book overhanging the edge, a mug turned slightly
- breaking the plane — one plant frond, a club head, a cable reaching toward the camera

One object crossing forward past the shelf line is worth more than four objects sitting flush.

Stack depth on **one side only**. The other side stays clean, or the frame turns to mush.

## Grouping rhythm

This is the biggest single improvement available over a typical dressed-office background, where shelves are loaded evenly from end to end like retail stock.

- **Odd numbers.** Cluster in threes and fives. Never pairs, never even rows.
- **Vary height inside a cluster.** Tall / mid / low, like a skyline. A cluster of three same-height objects is a row.
- **Gap between clusters** of roughly one object's width. The gaps are what make the clusters read as choices.
- **Mix orientation.** Some spines vertical, one or two books flat with a small object on top, one leaning at an angle against the upright.

Even loading reads as a warehouse. Clustered loading reads as somebody who lives there.

## Negative space

Reserve one area and leave it nearly empty: a stretch of bare brick between the window and the anchor, one shelf holding a single object, a blank patch of wall above the desk.

A room where every surface is loaded is exhausting to look at and reads as set dressing. One quiet area makes everything else look chosen. This is the most common thing missing from otherwise good personalized backgrounds.

## Read order

Decide, explicitly, what the eye hits and in what sequence. Write the prompt so the hierarchy is unambiguous.

1. **Hero** — one element only. Usually the identity object, because it is the one that makes somebody ask a question.
2. **Second** — the center anchor.
3. **Third** — the near unit and its readable contents.
4. **Fourth** — the window and its view.
5. **Texture** — plants, cables, coasters, the harmless clutter that has no meaning and isn't supposed to.

If everything is equally loud, nothing gets read.

**Rank by brightness and saturation, and enforce it.** The common failure is not too many objects — it is three co-equal bright shapes with no first among them: a white board, a saturated hero, and a glowing vessel all shouting at once. Density is fine. Ties are not.

Pick the hero, then actively demote the rivals: make the anchor smaller or hold it off pure white, keep the glowing object warm rather than bright, and let the hero keep the strongest colour in the room to itself. Only the hero sits in full key light.

## Framing

- 16:9, wide. Generate larger than needed; platforms scale down and never up.
- Camera at **seated eye height**. A room shot from standing height looks down at the desk and feels wrong behind a seated person.
- 2.5 to 3 meters back. Closer and the corner geometry collapses; further and nothing is readable.
- The desk edge crops the bottom of the frame and is the only soft element.
- **Show no floor.** Say the desk fills the lower quarter, runs the full width, and hides the floor completely. Visible floor is dead space, it drops the horizon, and it turns a room you are sitting in into a room you are looking at.
- **Make the ceiling register real.** A pipe run or conduit crossing the full width just below the ceiling costs one clause and is the difference between a building and a backdrop. It is the first thing generators drop when the prompt gets long — check it survived.
