# Object Casting

Casting, not decorating. Every object in the room is a line of dialogue about the person. Anything that says nothing about them gets cut and replaced with something that does.

## The portrait ratio

Aim for **at least half the cast drawn from life rather than work.**

This is the difference between the two backgrounds people actually admire and the ones they scroll past. A room built from business books, a company mug, and a work framework is a résumé pinned to a wall — technically personalized, emotionally empty. Add a golf bag, a hometown coastline, a 3D printer, and a Rubik's cube and the same room becomes a person.

The work objects establish competence. The life objects are what someone asks about. You need both, and the life half is the half people skip.

## The eight slots

| Slot | Question it answers | Draws from |
|---|---|---|
| **View** | Where are they from, or where would they rather be? | Hometown, heritage, a coast they sail, a range they climb, the city they left |
| **Center Anchor** | What is occupying their head right now? | A working framework, a model they argue for, a diagram they've drawn a hundred times |
| **Books** | What do they know? | Real titles they'd actually own, with author surnames |
| **Identity Object** | What do they do when they're not working? | Golf bag, instrument case, 3D printer, track bike, camera bodies, ceramic test tiles |
| **Brand** | Who do they represent? | Their employer or their own venture — wordmark only, two or three placements |
| **Ethos** | What do they believe about how work gets done? | A stated principle, a team phrase, something they repeat |
| **Desk Item** | What is the one tangible thing that's really theirs? | A specific notebook, a tool, a draft, a model |
| **Life** | What makes it a room somebody uses? | Plants at varied heights, a cork coaster, a coiled cable, a spare notebook |

Fill all eight. Seven filled slots and one blank is a hole; eight filled slots is a room.

## Distance is a casting decision

A slot is not filled until you have decided how big it is in frame. Anything small and far away renders as texture, however carefully it was named — a hand plane on a high shelf becomes an unidentifiable shape, and named book titles become smear.

Assign every cast slot a distance:

| Distance | What survives there | Put here |
|---|---|---|
| **Near** — foreground, large in frame | small text, fine detail, material texture | the identity object, the named books stacked flat, the desk item, the brand mug |
| **Mid** — the shelf register, sharp | large text, recognisable silhouettes | the center anchor, the letterboards, the brand bottle, the framed piece |
| **Far** — high shelves, deep corner, out the window | shape, colour, value only | the view, the plants, unnamed spines, the harmless texture |

**Distance is adjustable — move the furniture.** If a slot needs to be read and there is nowhere near enough to put it, the answer is not a better adjective. Bring a shelf unit onto the near plane and crop it, as in `room-architecture.md`. Books against a back wall are texture no matter how they are described.

**The identity object belongs near, not far.** It is the object that makes someone ask a question, and it earns a big place in frame — standing at the end of the shelving where the unit meets the desk, leaning against the wall below the window, or occupying the foreground corner. A golf bag on the floor at the edge of frame works. A golf ball on a high shelf does not.

## No object repeats another object

Every object has to say something no other object is already saying. A framed landscape photograph hanging beside a window that looks out on the same landscape is a duplicated sentence: it costs a slot, adds clutter, and tells the viewer nothing new. So does a second mug, a second framed motto in the same voice, or a shelf print of the hobby whose actual equipment is already in shot.

When two objects carry the same message, keep the one that is larger, nearer, or more specific and cut the other. The freed space becomes negative space, which the room usually needs more than another prop.

## Count the room

These rooms are full. Aim for **fifteen to twenty-five identifiable objects**, not counting plants and cables.

Anything under about a dozen reads as a showroom, which is the failure to guard against here — the instinct to make a room feel designed by removing things is exactly wrong. Group it and open one gap instead. If a draft names eight objects, it is not finished; go back to the slots and cast more.

## The specificity ladder

Each rung is a real improvement. Get to the top one.

| Rung | Example |
|---|---|
| Category — useless | "a nice view" |
| Named thing — usable | "a Newfoundland fishing village" |
| Named and staged — correct | "a Newfoundland fishing village, red and yellow houses stacked on a rock headland, grey water, low cloud" |

More:

- "some books" → *Inspired*, *The Mom Test*, *Range*, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* → the same four, two leaning against the upright, two stacked flat with a small object on top
- "a motivational poster" → "if you don't fail, you're not even trying" → the same phrase, hand-lettered white on navy, small frame, hung slightly low
- "sports gear" → a golf bag → a golf bag standing at the end of the shelving, clubs fanned, a headcover slouched over the driver
- "a mug" → a company-wordmark mug → the same mug on a cork coaster, handle turned away, sitting in the light spill

If the available context only supports the bottom rung for a slot, that's the signal to ask one targeted question rather than ship the weak version.

## The interview

Count the **life** slots — View, Identity Object, Ethos, Desk Item — that you can fill with a named specific from what you already know. Work context reliably fills Center Anchor and Brand and nothing else.

If fewer than two life slots are filled, ask once, compactly. One question, not a slot-by-slot interrogation:

> Four quick things so this room is actually yours: a place that means something to you for the window, a couple of authors or titles for the shelf, something you do outside work that has a physical object attached, and a phrase you'd genuinely hang on the wall.

Then build. Don't run a second round — the sign-off step catches the rest.

## Concepts are not objects

A framework, a methodology, an architecture — these have no physical form, so a generator invents one, and what comes back is a garbled corporate slide.

The fix is the medium, not the content. Hand-drawn in marker on a whiteboard renders as something a person stood up and drew; the same content described as a printed diagram renders as a screenshot taped to brick.

The content itself can be as formal as you like — flow arrows, a Venn, a loop, a line chart all render cleanly when the medium is marker on a whiteboard. What kills it is the word "poster", "slide", "printed", or "screenshot".

| Idea | Center anchor |
|---|---|
| a product process | whiteboard, marker: `PROBLEM → DISCOVER → DESIGN → DELIVER`, bullets under each, an outcome circled in the middle |
| a prioritization model | whiteboard, marker: three overlapping circles labelled business, user, technology, the intersection hatched |
| a decision philosophy | whiteboard, marker: `OBSERVE → ORIENT → DECIDE → ACT` as four boxes with a feedback arrow returning underneath |
| a growth thesis | whiteboard, marker: a rough line chart climbing, axis hand-labelled `IMPACT OVER TIME` |

## Ethos

One to three short phrases, and they carry more weight than their size suggests — they're the only place the person speaks in the first person.

Vary the medium so they don't read as a set: one hand-lettered framed print on the wall, one letterboard leaning on the window sill, one line of marker handwriting in the corner of the whiteboard.

Keep each to three to eight words. Never invent one — a generic motto is worse than an empty wall. If they haven't said anything that qualifies, drop the slot and let the negative space do the work.

## Brand

Two or three touchpoints, wordmark only, no tagline. One reads as product placement; three reads as somebody who works there.

The reliable set: a mug on the desk, a water bottle or tumbler on the shelf, and optionally a small framed wordmark on the wall. Keep all of them small.

## Trademarks

Do not reproduce a third-party trademark, sports-league crest, tournament mark, named consumer brand, or copyrighted artwork. Describe the category instead:

- a specific tournament print → "a framed vintage golf course map, red flag on the green"
- a named club brand → "a leather golf bag, unbranded, clubs fanned"
- a national flag mug → "a red-and-white enamel mug"

The exception is the person's own employer or venture. That wordmark is theirs to use.

## Do not invent

If a title, a hometown, a hobby, or a phrase was never stated and can't be reasonably inferred, don't supply one. An invented specific comes back in the sign-off, gets corrected, and costs more than the question would have.
