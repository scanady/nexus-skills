# Outcome Design Methodology

## Purpose

Outcome design is a pre-execution discipline for turning an intention, ambition, problem, decision, or proposed solution into a coherent design for creating a desired future state.

Its purpose is not to make every goal look formal. Its purpose is to reduce avoidable failure caused by unclear outcomes, weak measures, unsupported assumptions, implausible causality, hidden constraints, premature solution commitment, or unexamined trade-offs.

## Core model

A strong outcome design connects:

**Desired future state -> evidence of success -> necessary conditions -> behavior or system changes -> candidate mechanisms -> selected design -> progress evidence -> decision points -> downstream artifact**

The quality of the design depends on the integrity of these connections, not the amount of documentation.

## Six design gates

### 1. Outcome and value

Define what should be different, for whom, why it matters, what is excluded, and what must not be harmed.

A goal can be clear and still be a poor use of effort. Evaluate desirability, alignment, opportunity cost, and conflicts with other goals before optimizing the path.

### 2. Success and measurement integrity

Define evidence, baseline, target or threshold, time horizon, persistence, guardrails, and evidence sources.

Treat metrics as models of the outcome. Ask how they could mislead, be gamed, or improve while the real outcome deteriorates. Distinguish:

- Outcome measures
- Leading indicators
- Proxy measures
- Guardrails and countermetrics
- Activity and output measures

### 3. Reality and ownership

Ground the design in current conditions, evidence, constraints, dependencies, authority, incentives, prior attempts, and stakeholder behavior.

Distinguish:

- **Fact:** Supported by available credible evidence
- **Interpretation:** A reasoned explanation that may have alternatives
- **Assumption:** Treated as true for design purposes but insufficiently validated
- **Unknown:** Important information not yet available

### 4. Causal design

Use backcasting to identify conditions that must become true before the outcome can exist. Then compare approaches that use different causal mechanisms.

A theory of change should explain why the selected design is expected to produce the necessary behavior or system change, not merely list actions.

### 5. Uncertainty, feasibility, and risk

Prioritize assumptions by consequence and uncertainty. Focus first on assumptions that could reverse the design, invalidate the economics, create harm, or make the outcome infeasible.

Assess resources, authority, skills, time, data, technology, regulation, ethics, privacy, reversibility, and recovery.

### 6. Progress and downstream readiness

Define evidence that appears before the final outcome, review and decision points, stop or pivot conditions, and the next artifact required for execution.

A design is downstream-ready when a capable consumer can proceed without reopening foundational questions. It does not mean the outcome is guaranteed or externally validated.

## Adaptive questioning

The reviewer should not follow a static questionnaire. Each question should target the most consequential weakness in the current design.

A useful prioritization heuristic is:

**Review priority = consequence x uncertainty x decision sensitivity x tractability**

This is a qualitative heuristic, not a numeric score. It helps avoid spending time polishing low-value details while a major assumption remains untested.

## Proportional rigor

Increase depth with:

- Consequence of failure
- Cost and duration
- Irreversibility
- Number and vulnerability of affected people
- Regulatory, ethical, privacy, or safety exposure
- Novelty and technical uncertainty
- Dependence on uncertain human behavior
- Difficulty of measuring the outcome
- Difficulty of recovery

Reduce depth when the goal is low-stakes, reversible, familiar, and cheap to test.

## Learning under uncertainty

When the final design cannot yet be selected confidently, the appropriate output may be a design for learning rather than a design for implementation.

Identify:

1. The uncertainty most likely to change the recommendation
2. The smallest credible way to reduce it
3. The evidence threshold for choosing among alternatives
4. The decision that evidence will enable

This keeps experiments, research, prototypes, and pilots subordinate to the outcome rather than treating experimentation as the goal.

## Review discipline

A strong reviewer:

- Challenges claims without manufacturing disagreement
- Acknowledges settled thinking
- Does not reopen decisions without new evidence or contradiction
- Separates value judgments from factual claims
- Makes trade-offs visible
- Allows explicit acceptance of residual uncertainty
- Produces an artifact that shows both the design and why it exists
